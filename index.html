Core Differences: Classical FFT vs Quantum QFT

💻 Classical FFT (NumPy)

Representation: Operates on an array of $N$ classical complex numbers stored directly in memory registers.

Complexity: Computes all $N$ discrete frequency coefficients in $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ operations using divide-and-conquer (Cooley-Tukey).

Readout: Every frequency component $X_k$ is directly accessible and readable with zero uncertainty.

X_k = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j \cdot e^{-2\pi i j k / N}

⚛ Quantum QFT (Qiskit)

Representation: Encodes the $N$ values as probability amplitudes of an $n = \log_2(N)$ qubit superposition state.

Circuit Complexity: Requires only $\mathcal{O}(n^2) = \mathcal{O}((\log_2 N)^2)$ elementary quantum gates (Hadamard + Controlled-Phase rotations).

Measurement Limitation: The resulting state $|\psi\rangle$ cannot have all $N$ amplitudes read in one shot. Measurement collapses the state to a single basis state $|k\rangle$ with probability $|\alpha_k|^2$.

|\psi_{\text{out}}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j e^{+2\pi i j k / N}\right) |k\rangle

⚖ The Critical Convention Bridge

1. Normalization: Quantum statevectors must have unit norm $\sum |\alpha_j|^2 = 1$. Input $x$ is normalized by $||x||_2$.

2. Sign Convention: Standard QFT uses a positive phase exponent ($+i$). Standard forward FFT uses a negative exponent ($-i$).

The Bridge: To compare NumPy with Qiskit's `QFTGate`, we scale NumPy's `ifft` (which has positive exponent):

\text{Expected QFT} = \frac{\sqrt{N}}{||x||_2} \cdot \text{np.fft.ifft}(x)

📐 Mathematical Foundations & Convention Decoder

        


        

          

            
1️⃣ Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

            
The classical discrete Fourier transform converts a discrete sequence $x = [x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{N-1}]$ from the time domain to the frequency domain:

            
X_k = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j \cdot e^{-i \frac{2\pi j k}{N}} \quad (k = 0, \dots, N-1)

            
Notice the negative sign in the exponent, standard across electrical engineering, digital signal processing, and NumPy (`np.fft.fft`).

          


          

            
2️⃣ Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT)

            
The QFT is a unitary operator mapping basis states $|j\rangle$ to superpositions of basis states $|k\rangle$:

            
\text{QFT} |j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{+i \frac{2\pi j k}{N}} |k\rangle

            
When acting on an arbitrary quantum state $|\psi\rangle = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j |j\rangle$, the output amplitudes are:

            
y_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j \cdot e^{+i \frac{2\pi j k}{N}}

            
Notice the positive sign in the exponent, standard in physics and quantum information (Qiskit).

          


          

            
3️⃣ Why the Outputs Differ & How to Reconcile

            
Comparing NumPy's `fft(x)` directly with Qiskit's `QFTGate` yields an apparent mismatch due to two factors:

            
    
              
  • Sign inversion: NumPy's `fft` has $-i$; Qiskit's QFT has $+i$. However, NumPy's inverse FFT (`ifft`) uses $+i$!
    • 
              
  • Scale factor: NumPy's `ifft` multiplies by $\frac{1}{N}$, whereas QFT normalizes by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$.
    • 
            

            
\text{np.fft.ifft}(x)_k = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j e^{+i \frac{2\pi j k}{N}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \cdot y_k

            
Multiplying NumPy's `ifft` by $\sqrt{N}$ and normalizing by $||x||_2$ reproduces the QFT statevector exactly!

          


          

            
4️⃣ Quantum Circuit Architecture

            
The standard QFT circuit operates on $n = \log_2 N$ qubits using two elementary gate types:

            
    
              
  • Hadamard Gate ($H$): Creates equal superposition on target qubit: $H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.
    • 
              
  • Controlled-Phase Rotations ($R_k$): Introduces phase shifts conditioned on control qubits: $R_k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{2\pi i / 2^k} \end{pmatrix}$.
    • 
              
  • SWAP Gates: Reverses qubit bit-order at the end of the circuit to restore standard index ordering.
    • 
            

            
\text{Gate Count} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \text{ (Controlled-Phase)} + n \text{ (Hadamard)} = \mathcal{O}(n^2)

          


          

            
5️⃣ Asymptotic Complexity: Reality vs Myth

            
Classical FFT: Takes $\mathcal{O}(N \log N) = \mathcal{O}(n 2^n)$ operations. All $N$ coefficients are calculated and stored in memory.

            
Quantum QFT Circuit: Uses only $\mathcal{O}(n^2) = \mathcal{O}((\log_2 N)^2)$ elementary gates — an exponential speedup in unitary gate depth!

            
The Measurement Catch: You cannot read all $N$ amplitudes out of a quantum computer in $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ time. A single measurement gives one random basis state $|k\rangle$. QFT is useful as a subroutine inside quantum algorithms (e.g., Shor's factoring, Quantum Phase Estimation) where constructive interference concentrates probability on the desired answer.

          


          

            
6️⃣ Classical Simulation Limitations

            
This project uses Qiskit's `Statevector` simulator running on a classical CPU.

            
A classical simulator stores the full statevector of $2^n$ complex numbers in host RAM and multiplies matrices explicitly, taking $\mathcal{O}(2^n)$ time and memory. Therefore, timing Qiskit on a laptop does not demonstrate quantum advantage.

            
\text{Simulator Memory} = 2^n \times 16 \text{ bytes (exponential in } n)

Interactive Viva Flashcards (High-Frequency Examiner Questions)

        
        

          

            Q1: What is a qubit, and how does it store information differently from a classical bit?
            ▼
          

          

            A classical bit can only exist in state 0 or 1. A qubit is a two-level quantum system represented by a unit vector in a 2D complex Hilbert space: $|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle$, where $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}$ and $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. The coefficients $\alpha, \beta$ are probability amplitudes.
          

        


        

          

            Q2: Why must the input signal amplitudes be normalized before loading into Qiskit?
            ▼
          

          

            By the Born rule of quantum mechanics, the sum of probabilities of all possible measurement outcomes must equal 1 ($\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} |\alpha_j|^2 = 1$). Therefore, a classical vector $x$ must be normalized by its Euclidean ($L_2$) norm $||x||_2 = \sqrt{\sum |x_j|^2}$ so it can be physically represented as a pure quantum statevector.
          

        


        

          

            Q3: Why do NumPy's FFT and Qiskit's QFT outputs have opposite imaginary parts if compared directly?
            ▼
          

          

            NumPy defines the forward Discrete Fourier Transform with a negative phase exponent $e^{-2\pi i j k / N}$. Quantum mechanics and Qiskit define the QFT operator with a positive phase exponent $e^{+2\pi i j k / N}$. Because $e^{+i\theta} = (e^{-i\theta})^*$, direct comparison shows conjugate symmetry (opposite imaginary sign). To match them, we compare QFT with NumPy's inverse FFT (`np.fft.ifft`), which uses the positive exponent convention.
          

        


        

          

            Q4: If QFT uses only $\mathcal{O}((\log N)^2)$ gates, why can't we use it to replace FFT on classical audio or image files?
            ▼
          

          

            There are two major bottlenecks: 
            1) State Preparation: Loading an arbitrary classical vector into $n$ qubits requires $\mathcal{O}(2^n) = \mathcal{O}(N)$ operations in general.
            2) Measurement Readout: After QFT, the output exists as quantum amplitudes. When you measure the qubits, quantum state collapse yields only a single basis index $|k\rangle$, not all $N$ frequency values. Reading all $N$ values would require re-running and sampling the circuit millions of times, eliminating any speedup. QFT is meant as an internal subroutine in algorithms like Shor's or Quantum Phase Estimation, where interference allows reading the desired answer directly.
          

        


        

          

            Q5: Does our local Python execution timing prove that QFT is faster or slower than FFT?
            ▼
          

          

            No, absolutely not. We are running a quantum simulator on a classical laptop CPU. The statevector simulator must simulate $2^n$ quantum state amplitudes classically, requiring exponential time and memory $\mathcal{O}(2^n)$. The timing numbers in `summary.csv` are single-run wall-clock observations on a classical computer, not a benchmark of physical quantum hardware.
          

        


        

          

            Q6: What gates make up a QFT circuit, and why are SWAP gates necessary at the end?
            ▼
          

          

            A standard QFT circuit consists of Hadamard gates $H$ and Controlled-Phase rotation gates $R_k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{2\pi i / 2^k} \end{pmatrix}$. Applying these gates naturally leaves the resulting output qubits in reversed bit order (most significant qubit becomes least significant). Therefore, SWAP gates are applied across paired qubits ($\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ swaps) to invert the register back into natural binary order.
